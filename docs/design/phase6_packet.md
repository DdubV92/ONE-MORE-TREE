### 01. The Tall Woman Beyond the Trees

**Identity:** An impossibly tall, pale figure with a broad dark hat; her face never resolves. An original tall-woman apparition, using modern folklore imagery without claiming to reproduce a particular tradition.

**Eligibility and constraint:** Needs long vertical sightlines, partial trunk occlusion, and a quiet previous minute. Her height is noticed gradually; she never becomes a fully lit close-up model.

**Woods — five authored variations**

1. A pale strip between trunks becomes a sleeve when the wind moves everything except it.
2. Her hat remains visible above a low canopy while the rest of her stands behind a distant tree.
3. A soft three-note vocalization shifts between distant locations without growing louder or revealing a speaker.
4. You crest a rise and see her head above a rock that concealed an unexpectedly long body.
5. After you retreat, the treeline holds a silhouette closer to your former work spot, watching the abandoned axe marks.

**Outdoor camp — five authored variations**

6. Beyond the fence, a vertical pale shape is easy to mistake for a stripped trunk until its shoulder turns.
7. Her hat briefly interrupts the sky above the entrance; her feet remain hidden below the distant slope.
8. A lamp throws a long figure-like shadow across dirt, but its actual source stays outside the lit boundary.
9. From one camp corner she seems absent; from the next, the same hat appears behind a different trunk.
10. A slow fabric scrape travels along the far fence while a tall shadow passes between pools of safe light.

**Worker cabin with the crew — five authored variations**

11. A worker pauses at the window, then quietly resumes folding a blanket without explaining what they saw.
12. Above the curtain edge, a dark brim crosses a window that should be too high for a passerby.
13. The far wall receives a passing elongated shadow while everyone inside remains still.
14. A tall pale form is visible through a gap in the curtains only from the player's seated height.
15. All workers glance toward an upper window after a soft tap, but nothing enters and their beds remain usable.

**Private camper before bed — five authored variations**

16. A hat-shaped reflection appears in the camper glass, spatially aligned with a distant figure behind the player’s reflected room.
17. Looking outside reveals a pale sleeve far away; looking again shows only the fence and moving leaves.
18. The dog lifts its head at the three-note call, then presses against its bed instead of investigating.
19. Her silhouette crosses behind the distant cabin, taller than its roofline, without entering either building.
20. Closing the curtains leaves a slowly retreating shadow across them; reaching the bed always remains possible.

**Security cameras and microphones — five authored variations**

# APPENDIX C — THE FOREMAN: PHYSICAL HUNTER, ORIGINAL VISUAL DESIGN, AND THIRTY-SIX HUNT PATTERNS

## 7. THE FOREMAN — the failure-state physical hunter

### Appearance and first reveal

The Foreman stands about three meters tall when unfolded. At distance it resembles a soaked industrial worker wearing an enormous faded high-visibility coat. Nearer, the apparent reflective strips are uneven pale seams that catch light only at certain angles. Its shoulders are too narrow for its long arms, and its heavy hands hang below the knees.

The coat does not conceal a normal gait: the torso stays almost level while the legs take extremely long, sudden steps. When it crouches under a doorway, the upper body folds forward in several deliberate segments. Its head is usually tilted as though listening to a radio. Where a face should be is a recessed dark work-mask shape with no reliably visible eyes. Small brass-like survey tags hang around its throat and make a soft clinking sound when it is calm. When it is close, that sound stops.

Do not give it a clean explanatory origin. Its resemblance to company workwear makes the abandonment feel connected without confirming that the corporation controls it. It does not say “I am the monster” or deliver a villain speech.

First reveal: after the day-30 failure acknowledgement, the company's last boat recedes. A shape stands beside a distant survey marker previously too small to notice. It slowly straightens higher than the marker. Then it moves behind the utility building with a speed its size should not permit. The emergency radio gives a practical five-day rescue estimate. The game now tells the player clearly that ordinary lights and closed doors do not guarantee safety from this physical hunter.

### Final visual direction — grotesque industrial nightmare

Push the visual horror well beyond an ordinary tall figure in a coat. Use the oppressive scale, grotesque human resemblance, tactile material decay, and disquieting weight associated with dark storybook horror as broad inspiration. Little Nightmares is a mood reference only: create an original silhouette, anatomy, costume, face, animation, and sound identity. Do not copy its characters, environments, or assets.

**Silhouette:** when fully unfolded, roughly three meters high, with a raised upper-back mass under a hanging work coat, a narrow forward-slumped chest, and disproportionately long arms. At rest it seems shorter because it folds inward. Its hands approach shin height. A truck roof or cabin doorway reveals the actual scale. Keep recognizability in a distant dark silhouette before adding surface detail.

**Head and face:** the head sits too far forward under a rigid-looking, weather-deformed hood/collar shape. The upper face looks compressed inward, with layered pale folds suggesting features that never align like a normal person's. Reliable eyes remain difficult to locate. The lower jaw hangs slightly off-center; on a rare close side view, it opens at a disturbing angle beneath the familiar face plane, exposing a cramped uneven line of small teeth. This is an original anatomical design, not a borrowed monster mask. Avoid an obvious cartoon grin, glowing eye buttons, or a brightly lit full-face showcase in ordinary play.

**Hands:** long flattened fingers, thick knuckles, dark blunt nail-like ends, and wet stretched-glove surface detail. They can rest around a real door frame before the torso appears. One hand can settle against the floor during a justified low search; the other braces its body. The fingers are articulated, not procedural noodles and not additional collision-breaking reach. Skin/material detail should make a near glimpse feel tactile without needing gore.

**Body and clothing:** the coat is heavy, stained, and partly difficult to distinguish from the surface beneath it. Seams, folds, embedded throat tags, dark water lines, and sparse faded warning-color fragments create an industrial association. Some areas look rigid and dry; others adhere like damp fabric to the body. The upper-back shape changes subtly as the torso unfolds, through actual rigged geometry rather than a separate creature suddenly spawning beneath the coat.

**Palette and texture:** use dirty pale gray, bruised taupe, soot-black recesses, dull metal, and tiny remnants of faded industrial yellow/orange. Contrast material roughness and occasional wet sheen. Skin folds, thick cloth edges, door-contact wear, and long fingernails matter more than noise pasted over every surface. Avoid uniform glossy plastic, flat gray cylinders, or a saturated mascot palette.

**Movement:** the head preserves attention after the torso turns. The back unfolds before a fast pursuit. A planted hand shifts weight before a doorway fold. Long strides cover distance while the torso stays unsettlingly steady. During a close pause, only a small jaw adjustment, finger pressure, or cloth settling may move. Rare sharp movement is more disturbing after genuine stillness. All contact and hit reach remain tied to the actual body.

**Reveal discipline:** first the shape; later a hand on a frame; later the angle of the jaw; finally a rare justified very close encounter. Daylight can expose material ugliness without giving a complete explanatory model inspection. Do not hide every view in darkness, and do not replace suspense with constant close-up attacks. Keep the under-bed/closet encounters playable and evidence-driven.

**Blender-free production:** establish a reusable articulated Godot model/rig or appropriately licensed ready-to-use source adapted to an original design, with consistent LODs and camera silhouettes. Use the existing code-generated/modular geometry pipeline where needed. A deliberate stylized approximation is acceptable during implementation, but label art gaps honestly and do not claim final grotesque skin/face quality from a primitive capsule. Deliver a development-only turntable plus idle/listen/doorway/search/sprint previews, and inspect actual gameplay scale. No local Blender or local Claude setup is required.

### What it sounds like

Its slow search has long quiet gaps, small tag clicks, heavy foot placement, fabric drag, and occasional distant breathing. A sprint begins with a short intake followed by rapidly approaching impacts, not a musical cue that always announces every attack. During investigation, footsteps can stop outside a door while a hand slowly probes the frame.

Sometimes its throat produces a broken fragment of a genuinely heard worker phrase, sounding less like deliberate speech than a radio stuck between channels. These fragments are occasional and do not replace normal positional audio. Quiet does not prove it has left.

### Capabilities and limits

The Foreman is the sole fully tangible supernatural enemy. The twenty-six normal families remain elusive presences; you cannot catch, capture, kill, or farm them. You can physically encounter The Foreman, and that is dangerous: it can kill the player and workers. It is not a boss to defeat with axes or truck impacts.

It walks while searching, moves faster than the player in a short direct sprint, and can set up an ambush along a recently evidenced route. Sprinting has readable commitment and a recovery/search phase. It cannot maintain infinite perfect-speed pursuit through every obstacle. Breaking line of sight, changing floor/cover routes, and hiding can work. It cannot teleport into an occupied hiding place or run through collision.

Open road truck travel may outrun it; engines, lights, road choke points, dismounts, and loading stops create risk. The truck is neither an invincible bunker nor arbitrarily disabled whenever it becomes useful. If it can reach a stationary occupied vehicle, use a telegraphed authored door/window interaction and a chance to exit before lethal commitment. The creature cannot leap across the map to intercept a legitimately escaped truck.

### Senses and uncertainty

Use an actual perception model:
- Vision: field of view, distance, occlusion, light/exposure, movement, and confirmation time.
- Hearing: weighted in-world sound events such as engines, running, dropped logs, doors, dog barks, and worker panic.
- Evidence: last seen position/heading, recent noise origin with uncertainty radius, disturbed doors, and credible local traces.
- Search memory: places recently searched, likely cover near last contact, habitual routes only after observation, and decaying confidence.

The director may offer a broad search-sector hypothesis when the creature has lost contact, never the exact current player position, closet ID, or living worker coordinates. Hints have cooldowns, uncertainty, and information justification. It must sometimes search wrong places. A player quietly changing plans should genuinely reduce confidence.

Agent states: Dormant/Arrival, Roam, Notice, Investigate, Confirm, Stalk, Intercept, Chase, LostContact, Search, ShelterProbe, Disengage, Recover. Goals use a utility/behavior planner with committed actions and bounded replan frequency. No continuous perfect A* request aimed at the hidden player's actual transform.

### Hiding and close encounters

Implement hiding under appropriate beds, inside closets with visible slats/holes, behind utility storage, beneath selected dock structures above safe water, and other authored reachable cover. Real collision, camera pose, exit points, sound filtering, partial view, and interaction prompts are required. No hiding inside decorative walls or clipping under mattresses.

Enter/exit takes a short readable time and remains cancellable where physically sensible. Clothes/closet slats limit vision without turning the screen black. Under-bed views show shoes, dust, and the creature's legs if it actually passes nearby. Comfort options reduce forced camera movement.

Hiding is not a global invisibility flag. If it sees someone enter a closet, it knows that evidence and may check it. If it loses sight before the entry, it searches nearby possibilities rather than receiving the correct target. Staying still and quiet helps; opening the door, sprinting out, or a nearby noise can expose the position. No compulsory microphone input or breath-holding minigame is required.

A bed can support both ordinary sleep interaction and a separate Hide Under action. The player cannot accidentally choose sleep during a chase because prompts overlap. Survival rest does not freeze the creature or jump the calendar to safety.

Shelter doors slow or conceal rather than grant immunity in HUNT_ACTIVE. Entry/breach has clear cues and tested geometry. Former safe camper and cabin volumes still protect against abstract ordinary Terror catches, but The Foreman uses its explicit physical attack system. Communicate this transition, never silently reuse the old catch rule to kill a supposedly safe player.

### Uncanny hunt standard — escalation beyond a conventional stalker

This revision intensifies The Foreman without granting perfect information, unavoidable scripted kills, or constant loudness. It should feel as though an unfamiliar intelligence is trying to understand people by observing their work, voices, shelters, and habits. It does not become a talkative villain or a checklist of telegraphed attacks.

#### An almost-human body that becomes worse when understood

At a distance, retain the plausible work-coat silhouette. Do not reveal all proportions immediately. Let a familiar door, parked truck, dock railing, or standing worker establish its scale. Its lower arms are initially obscured by the coat; a slow reach later reveals the hand was hanging far lower than expected.

Its head and torso do not turn together. The shoulders may finish turning while the head remains aligned toward the last sound, then the head eases into position without an ordinary human glance. A stationary body can make one very small listening adjustment. Avoid twitching every few seconds: stillness makes the unusual movement matter.

When passing a low doorway, it folds through an authored articulated pose instead of crouching like an ordinary tall person. The coat pools and slides in the opening; one hand braces on the real frame. Collider shape and animation must support this physically. No limb travels through the wall, no body scales randomly to fit, and no sudden length growth is used to cheat reach.

Up close, the recessed mask area has uneven pale edges that can be mistaken for facial features until the viewing angle changes. Do not turn this into a bright, clean face reveal or a cartoon permanent grin. Even in a close attack the player may understand its hands, posture, and movement better than its expression.

#### The listening stop

Make a real sensory state out of listening. The small throat tags, cloth movement, and heavy steps stop when it pauses. At close range a quiet, involuntary-sounding air movement may remain. The stopped sound is a possible cue that it is attending, not a guarantee it knows where the player is.

During listening, it evaluates nearby actual sound events and looks toward plausible sources. It can turn the wrong way. If the player stays quiet after breaking line of sight, confidence decays. Do not poll the player's hidden transform just because the game wants a more frightening scene.

Use multiple listening poses and durations with bounded limits. Some stops end in departure, some in a different investigation, some in a supported search. Never train “tags stop = attack exactly two seconds later.”

#### False departures and believable returns

A search may sound as though it is ending because the hunter walks away on an actual route, moves into a quieter material zone, or stops behind a real wall. It may then wait or revisit nearby cover if evidence still justifies suspicion. Its body genuinely changes location; fake receding footsteps cannot conceal a monster still standing beside the player unless the sound is an explicit separate mimic event with proper limits.

Occasionally it fully leaves. A player who successfully escaped deserves that success. Limit a search to one false-departure branch before a meaningful change of area or evidence. No endless closet camping, infinitely renewed suspicion, or guaranteed return whenever the player exits.

It must not react to a hiding-exit button before the physical action creates sight or sound. Quietly leaving through a valid unseen exit can work. This is how terror stays tense rather than revealing an unfair input-reading system.

#### Voice fragments that mean something

It can reproduce short fragments it actually heard in-game: an ordinary loading instruction, a worker's farewell, a frightened request, or the boss's established dog-reassurance phrase. Store these as authored licensed/original voice-event references, not dynamically clone a real person's voice.

The fragment sounds mechanically incomplete: wrong breathing position, two people sharing one cadence, a syllable repeated too carefully, or an apparently friendly phrase spoken while its body does not move naturally. Use this rarely; silent searching remains its dominant behavior.

A witness or departed/dead person's familiar phrase can be especially disturbing, but actual worker state must remain clear. The hunter cannot know conversations it never heard merely to personalize a scare. It does not imitate a living worker in a way that silently changes roster identity.

#### Close searches with partial sight

Under-bed hiding should use the floor-level view intentionally. A foot plants, weight shifts, the coat edge drags, and one hand may touch the boards if its search plan has a valid reason to inspect low cover. Some searches pass by. Others check another bed first. If it saw the player enter, suspicion can legitimately be much higher.

Inside a slatted closet, the player may see only a coat seam, two fingers on the outside frame, and a partial head tilt. Let outside light remain visible through narrow gaps so the player can judge whether it moved. Never black out the screen for thirty seconds and call that suspense.

The closest nonlethal search can involve it leaning toward a different suspected sound while the player remains hidden nearby. That near miss is stronger when survival follows the actual perception rules rather than a hidden cinematic immunity roll.

If the player is discovered, preserve a short readable physical commitment where layout allows an alternate exit. Not every discovered hiding place guarantees escape, especially after observed entry, but no stored instant-kill callback may fire after a valid escape or safety transition.

#### The crew becomes part of the uncertainty

Workers whisper short useful information they genuinely know: last heard direction, who is nearby, which door was left open. A calm worker can go silent mid-message after seeing movement. Silence alone does not automatically mean death; the person might be hiding, unable to speak safely, or outside signal range if a real radio system supports it.

Fear can cause hesitation, a dropped object, a louder breath, or a rushed route decision. These have warnings, trait variation, and opportunities to regroup; the player does not lose someone because a random unannounced fear roll demanded spectacle. Avoid repeating the same panic behavior until it becomes comic.

The player can see or hear a genuine hunt of another worker from cover or a camera. It is a live encounter with possible escape, not a predeclared kill. A worker who breaks sight and reaches unobserved cover may survive. Confirm deaths through persistent actor state and later information, never remove a portrait solely for shock.

During the contract the dog can seek the owner or settle beside the camper bed. During the failure hunt it reacts only from its dock refuge: becoming still, orienting to an actual nearby sound, or settling in its shelter. It never accompanies the owner into cover, becomes a sacrifice, or reveals an exact hunter distance. A silent dock dog does not prove the hunter is gone.

#### The environment stops reassuring you

Ordinary Terrors become quieter or withdraw when the physical hunter is active nearby. Use the shared director to reduce competing apparitions and stingers. A normally busy camera wall can become disturbingly ordinary, followed by the first genuine view of something tangible.

Sparse environmental sound should be spatial and temporary. Do not mute the entire island every time The Foreman is close, making silence a perfect threat detector. Wind, insects, water, indoor fans, and engines can mask some cues while other ordinary sounds continue.

Daylight does not dismiss it. A clear view can be unsettling because the familiar coat, proportions, and speed remain wrong without darkness hiding them. It may search farther away during bright periods or choose cover for tactical reasons, but there is no automatic daytime immunity.

### Twenty-four additional hunt sequences — conditional real-time encounters

These extend the twelve existing Foreman close-encounter patterns to thirty-six. Each requires its own valid physical approach, perception/evidence justification, retreat or escalation branch, and cooldown. None is a scheduled compulsory death.

1. **The second set of steps:** after a real line-of-sight break, the player's slowing footfalls overlap the hunter's separate distant steps. The apparent mimicry ends when both stop at different moments.
2. **The loading instruction:** from beyond the utility wall it repeats a phrase it heard during a genuine earlier truck-loading interaction. The familiar instruction now comes from a body too tall for the doorway.

### Uncanny-hunt pacing and technical acceptance

Build the thirty-six physical-hunt patterns as reusable conditional plans within the agent, not thirty-six unrelated cinematic monster clones. Use a live event timeline, stable physical identity, actual collision, route reservations, animation contact, perception uncertainty, and bounded search persistence.

Across five days, vary the mix of distant observation, interrupted chores, worker concerns, quiet searches, near misses, and short acute peaks. Do not escalate every parameter monotonically until the game becomes constant sprinting. Brief recoveries and real successful escapes give the player enough confidence for the next uncertain decision to matter.

High intensity comes from a stronger inference and closer plausible threat, not unlimited volume. Maintain separate audio controls, subtitles for relevant cues, reduced flashes/startles, readable hide prompts, and adjustable camera motion. A muted TV cannot make physical navigation impossible, and accessibility settings do not secretly increase discovery probability.

Verify: no exact-position director leakage; visible sensing mistakes; no attack on the dog; no creature clipping through doors/vehicles/beds; reasonable routes after forest clearing; quiet exits that actually work; fake-departure limit; global startle refractory periods; no untelegraphed shelter-rule change before abandonment; no forced worker death; and no attack commitment executing after an actor has genuinely escaped. Record examples of suspenseful non-attacks as well as staged scares.

### Five days of escalation

Day 31 establishes search and hiding rules through readable close calls and generous initial separation.
Day 32 increases investigation variety and worker coordination pressure.
Day 33 uses observed routes to choose possible ambush sectors, with genuine uncertainty.
Day 34 extends some local searches and puts more pressure on noisy truck travel.
Day 35 emphasizes dock access, regrouping, and the rescue approach, without spawning the hunter directly onto the gangway.

These are pacing tendencies, not five guaranteed cutscenes or automatic omniscience upgrades. It can kill during any hunt day once rules have been taught. Surviving means smart movement and hiding, not simply waiting in a permanently safe camper.

### Defeat, persistence, and outcomes

Player death ends the current attempt, with reload of the most recent valid survival checkpoint/manual save according to the stated mode. Worker deaths persist in the current saved timeline; replaying a checkpoint restores that checkpoint honestly, never a mixed roster.

Save physical monster state, uncertainty/evidence, worker life/fear/hide state, the dog's single-identity dock-refuge/boarding state and position, truck cargo/occupants, continuous survival clock, rescue beacon, and ship state. Loading restores a small input grace without erasing evidence or dropping the hunter on the player's face.

Success ending: contract met before deadline, crew paid/reconciled, company extraction.
Survival ending: contract failed, surviving humans board the rescue ship; show who escaped, who departed earlier, and who died.
Player-death ending: no false “everyone escaped” if the boss died.
Optional post-success exploration remains safe from failure-state spawning; the monster cannot appear because leftover pit fuel is still burning.
